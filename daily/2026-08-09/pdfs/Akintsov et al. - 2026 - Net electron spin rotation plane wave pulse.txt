We compute the spin rotation that survives after a relativistic electron has crossed a plane-wave
laser pulse of finite duration. In the interaction picture built on the exact g = 2 evolution, the
Thomas–Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation becomes parallel transport by a connection with constant coefficients on the polarization plane, and the pulse enters only through the closed curve that
the transverse vector potential traces there. The net rotation is the holonomy of that connection:
an angle − 21 a2e A about the propagation direction, with ae = (g − 2)/2 the anomaly and A twice the
signed area enclosed by the curve. That area is the spin angular momentum the pulse carries per
unit area, so the rotation measures the helicity of the light. Reduction of the residual dynamics to
a rotation coupled through the anomaly alone is an exact result of the 1960s [Ternov, Bagrov, and
Klimenko, Sov. Phys. J. 11, 29 (1968); Bagrov and Gitman, The Dirac Equation and its Solutions
(De Gruyter, Berlin, 2014), Sec. 5.3], which yields two closed-form cases; the area law is the general
second order that those two cases bound. The same area governs the orientation memory of a neutral
magnetic dipole [Oblak and Seraj, Phys. Rev. D 109, 044037 (2024)] with a coupling of order unity.
For a charged electron on a Volkov orbit the coupling g/2 cancels identically, which suppresses the
rotation by 1.3 × 10−6 and leaves a channel with no g-independent part. We verify the cancellation
at g = 2 over 180 pulse configurations and the area law over 89 more. Finite focusing restores that
part at second order in 1/kw0 , and it exceeds the anomalous signal unless w0 ≳ 16λ at γ = 10, or
270λ at γ = 1.

I.

INTRODUCTION

Spin polarization of electrons in intense laser fields is
usually treated as a radiative process: the spin flips when
a photon is emitted, and the flip rate follows the structure of the field [1–6]. That channel closes as the quantum parameter χ becomes small, its probability per pulse
falling as αa0 χ2 N for N cycles at normalized amplitude
a0 . The spin keeps moving below it. Inside a pulse with
a0 ∼ 10 the Thomas–Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi (BMT)
equation [7] turns the rest-frame polarization through radians, and nearly all of that rotation is undone while the
pulse passes.
What survives is a holonomy. For a plane-wave background, Aµ = Aµ (η) with η = k · x the light-front phase,
and for a pulse of finite duration, the rotation surviving after the field has gone is Θnet = − 21 a2e A, where
ae = (g − 2)/2 is the anomalous magnetic moment and A
is twice the signed area that the vector potential encloses
in the polarization plane. Every property of the pulse
acts through the curve that a⊥ (η) traces in that plane,
and at leading order through its area alone. The electron

The area carries a physical name. In the Coulomb

Two of the three ingredients of this result are in print,
in two literatures that have never cited one another, and
Sec. IV sets out the division in full. The exact spin problem for an electron with an anomalous moment in a plane
wave was solved by the Tomsk school in the 1960s [8–11]
and rediscovered in a gravitational context three decades
later [12]: the residual dynamics is a rotation whose coupling is the anomaly alone, the first order in the anomaly
is algebraic in a⊥ (η), and exactly two profiles integrate
in closed form. Separately, the net reorientation of a
precessing magnetic dipole after a burst of radiation has
passed is known to be of second order in the coupling and
to equal the helicity of the burst [13], a statement phrased
as a holonomy in the memory literature [14]. Neither line
supplies the general second order for a pulse of arbitrary
shape, and neither treats a charged relativistic particle,

The rotation axis is the propagation direction in the

that axis leaves the momentum and the helicity statement lapses. Linear polarization gives no net rotation

IV.

RELATION TO EARLIER WORK

Three separate lines of work reach parts of Eq. (10),
and the boundaries between them and the present paper
are sharp enough to be drawn one by one.

A.

The exact plane-wave spin problem

The Dirac–Pauli equation for a particle with an anomalous moment in a plane wave of arbitrary profile was
solved exactly in the 1960s. Ternov, Bagrov and Klimenko treated a circularly polarized wave and read off
the shift of the spin precession frequency caused by the
anomaly [8]. Chakrabarti obtained the polarization directly in integrated form for a class of fields F = F (k · x),
with an anomalous magnetic moment and an anomalous
electric dipole moment both arbitrary [9]. The material
is collected in the monograph of Bagrov and Gitman,
Sec. 5.3 of Ref. [11] and Chap. 8 of the earlier edition [10],
and four of its statements bear directly on this work.
First, after the Volkov factor is separated the residual spinor equation is driven by the anomalous moment
alone, with the wave entering through a′⊥ (η), which is
the content of Eq. (5) above, in quantum form and three
decades earlier. Second, the first order in the anomaly
is written there in closed form, and the operator is an
algebraic function of a⊥ (η) and not an integral of it, so
that our Eq. (8) is one line away from that expression,
although the asymptotic step and the statement about
a pulse are not taken there. That step is short, and it
was not taken in that line. Third, linear polarization of
arbitrary profile is solved exactly, the evolution operator being a function of the instantaneous a⊥ (η). For a
pulse that operator is the identity, which is the exact zero
quoted in Sec. III B. Fourth, a potential modulus with an
arbitrary phase function is solved exactly, and the answer
depends on the total phase swept and not on the phase
function, which is Eq. (12) together with its independence of chirp. The same reduction was found independently three decades later, for electromagnetic and gravitational pp-wave backgrounds, by Balakin, Kurbanova
and Zimdahl [12]. They state explicitly that nothing rotates at g = 2.
One thing that line does not contain is the general second order. Bagrov and Gitman say plainly that two cases
are known for which the residual equation is solved exactly, and those are the two just listed; neither an asymp-

totic limit for a pulse of finite duration nor an observable
net rotation is formulated anywhere in that literature.
The language throughout is one of wave functions, quantum numbers and almost-periodicity. Equation (10) is
the second order for an elliptical curve of arbitrary profile, and the two exactly solvable cases are its degenerate limits: for linear polarization the curve collapses and
A = 0, while for a circular plateau the area is known in
closed form and Eq. (10) is the leading term of Eq. (12).
One consequence of that reading is worth recording.
In the Dirac–Pauli problem the anomalous magnetic and
electric moments enter through a single modulus and a
constant mixing angle, and a constant rotation in the
transverse plane leaves the signed area unchanged. Equation (10) therefore holds for a particle carrying both, with
ae replaced by the modulus of the combined anomalous
moment.

B.

The vanishing at g = 2

That a plane wave leaves the asymptotic spin of a
g = 2 electron untouched is old and well recorded. Kupersztych derived the underlying gauge mechanism in
1976 [22]. Aleksandrov et al. found that the spin change
in a linearly polarized
pulse is proportional to the elecR
tric pulse area E dt [31], which is zero for any pulse
with a finite envelope. For arbitrary polarization the
same statement follows in one line from the Volkov solution [21]. Ilderton, King and Tang proved it in the
quantum theory and wrote the first order in the anomaly
explicitly: theR amplitude for reversing the spin state
is |c− | = 12 dη µb (a′x − ia′y ) , with µb their phasedependent anomaly and the light-front integral running
over the whole pulse [34]. The result is stated verbatim
in the review literature [5, 6]. Section III A adds nothing
to it and cites it as the starting point.
The delimitation against Ref. [34] needs care, because
their vanishing condition is parity: the amplitude is zero
when the potential is an even function of the phase.
Unipolarity enters only in the weak-field limit. For a
pulse of zero electric area that is odd in the phase, produced by a shifted carrier-envelope phase, a chirp or an
asymmetric envelope, their first order survives and can
exceed the holonomy. By how much we have not evaluated. A constant µb returns µb ∆a⊥ = 0 by Eq. (1), so
what is left of their integral is the variation of µb across
the pulse, and that variation is itself of order χ. Their
expression is first order in a dressed anomaly µb (χ). Ours
is second order in the vacuum anomaly, and it does not
vanish when theirs does. The two are complementary.
The frame common to both, that non-radiative effects
dominate at small χ, is theirs.

C.

Area, helicity and memory

The functional A and its identification with helicity are
also in print, in a literature with no overlap with strongfield physics. Oblak and Seraj computed the net reorientation of a magnetic dipole after a burst of radiation
has passed. They found a second-order term equal to the
signed area swept by the transverse potential, and they
identify that area as the optical helicity of the burst [13].
Seraj and Neogi phrase memory observables of this kind
as holonomies [14]. Up to the coupling constant, their
nonlinear term and Eq. (10) are the same expression,
and we take both the area law and the helicity reading
from them, so that Sec. III D is written accordingly.
The physics is different, and the difference is the
point. Their test body is a neutral, static, nonrelativistic dipole obeying Ṁ i = k F ij Mj , and the coupling k
is the full gyromagnetic ratio, a number of order unity:
there is no charge, no orbit, no Thomas precession and
no g factor. Substituting an electron into that formula
with k = g/2 overestimates the rotation by a factor
(g/2)2 /a2e = 7.4 × 105 . For a charged relativistic electron
on an exact Volkov orbit the terms proportional to g/2
cancel identically against the boost and Thomas parts
of the generator, as Eq. (5) shows, and what remains
couples through ae alone. Hence their result cannot be
applied to an electron before that reduction is proved,
and ours does not follow from theirs. The reverse implication also fails. Their formula carries a term linear in
the field, the radial magnetic field at null infinity, which
is identically zero for an exact plane wave.
The suppression is not a defect. A g-independent background of the same geometric form is absent by Eq. (4),
so in an ideal plane wave the entire net rotation is anomalous. The factor 1.3 × 10−6 is the price of a channel with
nothing to subtract. Section VI shows how a finite waist
puts the background back.

D.

Schemes that do rotate the spin

Two published mechanisms produce a net rotation of
the same observable and must not be confused with this
one. Wei et al. rotate the transverse polarization of a relativistic beam through an arbitrary angle with a copropagating chirped or subcycle pulse [35]. Their pulses are
temporally asymmetric, which is a⊥ (+∞) ̸= a⊥ (−∞)
and breaks Eq. (1) at order a0e . The dominant coupling
in their analytic estimate is the kinematic 1/γ and not
the anomaly. Their result is chirp dependent while ours
is not, and there is no contradiction. What depends
on the chirp there is the accumulated precession phase,
whereas the invariant here is the net rotation relative to
the momentum, that is, the difference between the precession phase and the momentum phase. Tikhomirov
showed that a phase velocity vph ̸= c produces a spin
precession exceeding radiative effects by orders of magnitude [36, 37]; that effect vanishes at vph = c, so it con-

firms the plane-wave theorem from the other side and
identifies a medium as a way of breaking it.

E.

What is new here

Four items remain, and they are the claims of this paper. (i) The net rotation for a pulse of finite duration
and arbitrary elliptical profile is − 21 a2e A, Eq. (10), which
is the general second order that the two known exact
cases bound. (ii) The geometric reading: holonomy of a
connection with constant coefficients on the polarization
plane, a signed area evaluated by the non-Abelian Stokes
theorem, and a Z2 grading that fixes the remainder at
O(a4e ), the axis tilt at O(ae ) and the helicity change at
O(a6e ). (iii) The transfer of the area mechanism from a
neutral dipole to a charged relativistic electron, where
the correct coupling is the anomaly and not g/2. That
is the 1.3 × 10−6 suppression, and it is also the absence
of a g-independent background. (iv) Invariance under
reparameterization of the phase, together with the quantitative bound on finite focusing of Sec. VI. Everything
else in Secs. II and III we take from the works named
above.

VI.

FINITE FOCUSING

Equation (10) rests on A = A(η). Every real beam has
a waist, and in a focused pulse the spin moves already
at g = 2. The failure of the Lawson–Woodward theorem in a focused beam [40, 41] leaves a residual momentum transfer. The hierarchy of spin contributions in the
diffraction parameter ε = 1/(kw0 ) was set out qualitatively in Ref. [34], and a helicity change of up to 10% during ponderomotive scattering was reported in Ref. [42].
Neither work gives the exponent.
We modeled a focused Gaussian pulse with the Lax–
Louisell–McKnight expansion carried to third order in ε,
closing the Coulomb gauge at the retained order [43, 44],
and integrated the Lorentz and BMT equations in laboratory time. The Maxwell residuals of that model and the
systematic spread over its one free constant are tabulated
in Sec. S5 of the Supplemental Material. The observable
is the spin rotation relative to the momentum: the exit
reference triad is carried from the entrance triad by the
pure boost u0 → uf , so the kinematic transport that accompanies ponderomotive deflection is subtracted. Five
triads with anomalies ae ∈ {0, ±h, ±2h}, h = 0.05, then
share one orbit, and five-point differences give the rotation vector as r = r0 + ae r1 + a2e r2 .

escape, a particle with a larger anomaly, is closed by the
mass: the relevant parameter is ae a0 ∝ ae me /m, which

the required control of the impact parameter is realistic
is an experimental question we have not addressed.

VIII.

SCOPE AND OUTLOOK

Every hypothesis behind Eq. (10) can be broken on
purpose. Photon emission and radiation reaction spoil
u(+∞) = u0 , and radiation reaction brings in a dimensionful time, so γ and ω reenter. The closest published mechanism turns the spin toward the propagation
axis of a circularly polarized field at first order in ae ,
and it is emission that keeps the transverse spin phased
there [50]. Spin-dependent radiative deflection acts in
the same regime [51]. A standing wave adds a second
null direction, so that the two little algebras together
generate the whole Lorentz algebra and the ordering survives at g = 2 [52, 53], while a plasma gives k a mass,
k 2 = ωp2 , and a first-order rotation returns with weight
(ne /nc )/(ae a0 ), already of order 0.5 at ne /nc = 6 × 10−3
and a0 = 10. That rotation depends on the electron energy and on the carrier frequency, where the holonomy
does not, and it disappears as vph → c [36, 37]. In addition, self-interaction in a strong plane wave carries a

spin dynamics of its own at the same order in α [54]. We
integrated none of these cases. The estimates come from
the standard scalings collected in Refs. [5, 46].
However, a dressed anomaly leaves the structure intact
but not the derivation: the cancellation of Eq. (8) takes
ae outside the
R integral, so a phase-dependent ae (χ(η))
would leave ae (η) n̂ ∧ da⊥ ̸= 0 and restore a first-order
term [34, 55]. At χ ≃ 2×10−4 the dressing is far too weak
to matter, but the statement is exact only for constant
ae , and outside the locally constant field approximation
a local anomaly is not defined at all [20].
Two uses survive the negative budget of Sec. VII. The
identical zero at g = 2, and the identical zero at any
g for linear polarization, give structure-preserving spin
integrators [56, 57] a reference that needs no converged
solution to compare against, and the tolerance scan of
Sec. S4.3 shows what such a test looks like in practice.
The classical result is also a well-posed question at the
quantum level, where the exact Dirac–Pauli solutions in
a plane wave [9, 11, 19] make it tractable. We expect
an O(a2e ) geometric term in the spin-flip amplitude of
Ref. [34] at the order beyond the one written there, with
a coefficient predicted by Eq. (10) and no free parameter.

S3.1.

Reparameterization of the phase

Equation (S17) contains η only through a⊥ (η). Under
a monotone reparameterization η → s(η) that leaves the

What drops out is the rate, and only the rate, so the

fixed a0 enlarges the curve, adds turns and raises A, so

S3.2.

Independence of the electron energy

The null rotation generated by N1 = k ∧ e1 leaves k
and κ fixed, shifts u0⊥ by −cκ e1 for a parameter c, and
changes the polarization vectors only by terms ∝ k µ ,
so that the field tensor itself comes back unchanged,
N f N −1 = f , which is stronger than gauge equivalence.
Choosing c = u01 /κ brings any geometry to head-on,
the net map is conjugated, Λnet → N Λnet N −1 , and a
rotation angle is a conjugation invariant. That disposes
of the transverse momentum. The light-front projection
κ needs a different argument, because κ = k · u is a
Lorentz invariant and no boost removes it. It appears
solely through the scale chosen for k, hence as a rescaling η → η/κ of the phase, to which the holonomy is
insensitive by Sec. S3.1. Thus the inputs that remain are
the curve C and the number ae , and γ is not among them.
Measured values agree. For circular polarization at
a0 = 5 and N = 1 the net rotation is 5.621424 × 10−5 rad
at γ = 1, 10, 102 , 103 and 5.621423 × 10−5 at γ = 104 , six

anomaly switched on, an initial transverse momentum

and a copropagating geometry with κ = 0.025 gives the
same. The g = 2 null test of the main text was run to

the propagation direction of the wave in the electron rest
frame to four digits (measured).

S3.3.

Why the storage-ring factor ae γ does not
apply

In a storage ring the field is static in the laboratory,
and the ratio of the anomalous precession frequency to
the cyclotron frequency is ae γ [16, 38, 39]. That ratio
compares rates at one instant of laboratory time, and
the enhancement comes from the rest-frame field being γ
times the laboratory field at fixed B. Neither ingredient
survives here. The strength of a plane wave in the rest
frame is measured by the invariant a0 , which no boost
changes, and the g = 2 part of the precession, which is
what ae γ multiplies, gives a net rotation of exactly zero.

15
The product is ae γ · 0 = 0.
This is a question we have been asked more than once,
so we state the practical form: the enhancement is real,
but it applies to a quantity that vanishes identically in
this geometry, and the surviving quantity is O(a2e ) with
no compensating factor, so that the one configuration in
this paper where ae γ does return is the static field superposed on the wave in Sec. S7, where the added field tensor
no longer lies in k ∧ (·) and the argument of Sec. S2.1 has
nothing to act on.

Systematic error of the field model

Three variants of the field were run through identical

S5.8.

What was not computed

Exact nonparaxial fields, such as the complex-source
or Sewell–Barnett solutions, were not used and the LLM
model was never checked against them [60], only the
Gaussian envelope was run in the focused geometry, and
neither cos2 nor an isodiffracting envelope, which is the
one construction that would remove the εp /σ q terms of
Sec. S5.2, was tried at all. Radiation reaction was not
integrated in the focused geometry any more than in the
plane wave. Averaging over the focal volume and over
the beam spectrum was not done: every number above
is for a single electron at a fixed impact parameter with
exact synchronization, and the measured spread over b
within one waist is a factor of 20 in |r0 |. Ellipticity at
φ0 ̸= 0, a transverse offset along y, oblique geometries,
chirp, polarization gating, and higher transverse modes
were not scanned.

Of the entries in the table, radiation reaction deserves
the longest remark, because it does more than shift a
number. The Landau–Lifshitz force carries the classical electron time τ0 = 2e2 /3mc3 , and once a dimensionful constant sits in the equation of motion the argument of Sec. S3.2 collapses, since τ0 ω and γ can then
be formed and the answer is free to depend on both. Its
weight can be estimated from the radiated fraction, a
relative distortion 2RC of the curve traced in the rest
frame against a holonomy of relative size ae a0 , so that
ΘRR /Θnet ∼ 2RC /(ae a0 ). Evaluated on the rows of Table S9 that estimator is 2 × 10−3 at γ = 1, a0 = 10,
N = 8 and about 4 at γ = 103 , where the correction has
overtaken the quantity it corrects. It is also 6 × 10−2 at
the best classical point of that table and 0.30 one row
below it, at γ = 10, a0 = 75, N = 8. A correction of
six to thirty percent sits badly with a law quoted to a
percent, and only an integration of the Landau–Lifshitz
equation would settle the matter. We did not perform
one.
The published mechanism closest to this one is the rotation of the spin toward the propagation axis by the
anomalous moment in a circularly polarized field [50].
That effect is linear in the anomaly, and the reason is instructive here: repeated emission re-prepares the transverse spin with a phase locked to the field, so the cancellation of Sec. S2.2 never gets the closed loop it needs.

Switch the emission off and the linear term disappears
again.
The first order also returns with no radiation involved
at all. A counterpropagating wave introduces a second
null direction k2 , and the two little algebras iso(2)k1 and
iso(2)k2 together generate the whole of so(1, 3), so the
ordering in Eq. (S7) survives even at g = 2 [52, 53]. A
medium does it more cheaply. The dispersion relation
gives k a mass, k 2 = ωp2 , the little group stops being
ISO(2), and the nilpotency on which Eq. (S11) rests is
gone. This is the effect Tikhomirov identified [37], and
its weight relative to the holonomy is (ne /nc )/(ae a0 ),
already of order 0.5 at ne /nc = 6 × 10−3 and a0 = 10.
Residual gas at that density competes with the effect on
equal terms.
A dressed anomaly ae (χ) leaves the structure intact,
and it turns the result into a measurement. Since Θnet
is quadratic in ae and no other plane-wave mechanism
produces a net rotation, the transverse polarization angle measures the square of the field-dressed anomalous
moment [34, 55] with no g-independent background to
subtract. One qualification is internal to the derivation. The cancellation of Sec. S2.2 takes ae outside
the
R integral, so a phase-dependent ae (χ(η)) would leave
ae (η) n̂ ∧ da⊥ ̸= 0 and restore a first-order term. At
the working point χ ≃ 2 × 10−4 the dressing is far too
weak for that to matter, but the statement is exact only
for constant ae . How the area law is modified when χ
approaches unity, where ae (χ) is not defined outside the
locally constant field approximation, we did not analyze.
